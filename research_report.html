FRC 2026 Shooter Optimization · Research Report

Big-dumper auto-aim solver · 2026-05-23 → 2026-05-24
Author: Yihan Zhu (justaboringname)

Abstract

This report documents the design of an auto-aim pipeline for an FRC 2026 big-dumper-style shooter. Given the horizontal distance to the hub, the solver outputs flywheel RPM, hood roller RPM, and hood angle. After six algorithm iterations, the final choice is V3.1: centered-landing + physics-calibrated cost (\(w_E \cdot (90° - \text{entry}) + w_V \cdot v^2 + w_T \cdot \text{TOF}^2\), where \(w_T = 1.1\) is derived from vortex-shedding physics; see the V3.1 calibration section). The V4 family of robust-center methods (4414-style), while theoretically more elegant, was dropped due to plateau-flip jitter. In burst simulation V3.1 achieves 100% hit rate and spread ≤ 0.69 m across 1.5–5 m (the original V3 with w_T=200 managed only 94% hit rate and 1.19 m spread at d=5 m), and because the high-arc plateau is nearly monotonic in (d, hood) space, the polynomial fit RMSE is 4× smaller than V3 (hood 0.10° vs 0.40°) — plug the fit straight into the RIO. A companion interactive HTML simulator (Alpha Sim) supports V3 vs V3.1 A/B switching.

Deliverable shape (see §5.2): one closed-form function aim(distance) → (hood angle, flywheel surface speed in m/s); at competition a single gain coefficient kSpeed calibrates landing distance — no parameter-by-parameter retuning. Surface speed is wheel-diameter-independent (a friend's new robot may use a different wheel); any RPM appearing in this report is just the equivalent reference value for this project's 0.1016 m wheel.

1. Problem Statement

Game: FRC 2026 REBUILT presented by Haas. Scoring is done by throwing FUEL (5.91" / 15 cm high-density foam balls, 215 g) into the hexagonal opening at the top of the HUB (across-flats 41.7" / 1.06 m, front edge 72" / 1.83 m off the floor).

Robot: big-dumper architecture, holding 100 FUEL at once and firing them all within 3 seconds (~33 balls/s). Mechanical configuration:

  • Flywheel: 4″ (0.1016 m) compliant wheel, Kraken X60 direct drive (gear ratio 1.0, TBD)
  • Hood roller: 2″ (0.0508 m) back-plate follower wheel, Kraken X60, gear ratio TBD
  • Exit height: 0.50 m above the floor (measured; early design exploration used 0.46 m — the final delivered coefficients were recomputed at the measured 0.50 m, see §5.1)
  • Hood travel: 25° – 85°

Distance range: per the 2026 manual, the HUB sits at the center of the alliance zone, 4.03 m from the alliance wall; the alliance zone is 4.03 m deep. Realistic big-dumper shooting distances are 1.5 – 5 m.

Optimization objective: given horizontal distance \(d\), output \((\omega_\text{fly}, \omega_\text{hood}, \theta)\) maximizing hit probability under real mechanical noise; when multiple candidates have roughly equal hit probability, prefer the one with lower \(v_\text{exit}\) (energy saving).

Hard constraints:

  • The trajectory must pass through the hex opening (conservatively, the inscribed-circle radius \(a - r_\text{ball} = 0.530 - 0.075 = 0.455\) m)
  • \(\theta \in [25°, 85°]\); \(\omega_\text{fly}, \omega_\text{hood} \le 6000\) RPM
Soft objectives (mutually conflicting — the core difficulty of the project):
  • Lower \(v_\text{exit}\) → energy saving (Kraken power management)
  • Shorter TOF → smaller ball-to-ball dispersion within the 3-second burst
  • Controlled trajectory peak height → soft foam balls avoid "rim bounce out"
  • Reasonable entry angle (but nothing extreme — foam balls don't rebound like baseballs)

2. Physics Model

2.1 Trajectory ODE

State vector \(\mathbf{s} = [x, y, v_x, v_y]^\top\), quadratic air drag plus gravity:

$$\dot{x} = v_x, \quad \dot{y} = v_y$$ $$\dot{v}_x = -k\, |\mathbf{v}|\, v_x, \quad \dot{v}_y = -g - k\, |\mathbf{v}|\, v_y$$

where the drag constant \(k = \rho C_d A / (2m)\), \(A = \pi r_\text{ball}^2\).

The aerodynamic drag coefficient is fixed at \(C_d = 0.50\), chosen from the volleyball aerodynamics literature. The Reynolds number here, \(\text{Re} = v \, d_\text{ball} / \nu \approx 10^5\), sits in the volleyball's subcritical regime (critical Re ≈ 220–270 k), where measured \(C_d\) is 0.4–0.5.

Numerical integration: scipy.integrate.solve_ivp RK45 + event detection, terminating on the downward crossing of \(y = y_\text{target}\) (direction = −1).

2.2 Dual-roller launcher kinematics

No-slip rolling constraints: \(v_\text{top} = V - \omega r\), \(v_\text{bot} = V + \omega r\), inverted to give

$$V = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_\text{top} + v_\text{bot}), \quad \omega = (v_\text{bot} - v_\text{top}) / (2 r_\text{ball})$$

Stage 1 fixes \(\omega = 0\). The Magnus interface is already stubbed for Stage 2.

2.3 Target geometry

The 41.7" hex is approximated by its inscribed circle (apothem), minus the ball radius. Hit condition:

On the downward crossing of \(y = 1.829\) m, \(|x_\text{hit} - d| \le 0.455\) m.

3. Algorithm Evolution

The aim solver went through five iterations. Each round fixed a failure mode of the previous one — the process illustrates how a plausible-looking objective produces degenerate solutions when the search space is unconstrained, and how to fix it.

V1: 2D unconstrained FAILED

Decision variables \((v_\text{exit}, \theta)\), cost \((90° - \text{entry})^2 + \beta v_\text{entry}^2\). scipy Nelder-Mead.

Failure modes: (1) \(\theta \to 89°\), lobbing the ball straight up; (2) \(\theta_\text{max}\) not enforced; (3) after adding \(\theta\) bounds, \(v\) got pushed to max and the ball landed on the far edge of the hex instead of the center.

V2: 1D θ-sweep + centered-landing constraint STABLE

Promote "land at the hub center" from a soft cost to a hard constraint. For each \(\theta\), solve the unique centered-landing \(v\) with brentq, then optimize over \(\theta\) alone in 1D.

The drag-free analytic formula seeds the root-finder: \(v^2 = g d^2 / (2 \cos^2\theta (d \tan\theta - \Delta h))\).

V3 → V3.1: dispersion and energy terms PRODUCTION

V2's entry-only cost pinned \(\theta\) at the upper limit. Add dispersion (TOF²) and energy (v²) terms:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = w_E \cdot (90° - \text{entry}) + w_V \cdot v^2 + w_T \cdot \text{TOF}^2$$

\(w_E\) (linear)
1.0 — small (foam balls don't bounce)
\(w_V\) (v²)
3.0 — large (energy saving)
\(w_T\) (TOF²)
1.1 — physics-derived (vortex shedding, below)
The original V3 (w_T = 200) was a failure. The TOF² term was treated as a vague "longer flight = more dispersion" proxy, but 200 was pulled out of thin air. Burst simulation later showed V3's solutions hitting only 94–98% with 0.96–1.19 m spread at d=3–5 m, far worse than a hand-tuned high-arc scheme. V3.1 (w_T = 1.1, derived at the end of this section) fixes this: 100% hit rate at every distance, spread ≤ 0.69 m.

Diagnosis: why w_T = 200 was wrong — the two faces of TOF²

The physical meaning of the TOF² term is that the longer the flight, the further the ball drifts laterally from the nominal landing point. But that "drift" has two sources, and their dependence on hood angle points in opposite directions:

  • v noise (mechanical jitter ±3% v): drag accumulates over time, so a long TOF amplifies \(\Delta v\) into \(\Delta x\). TOF² is the correct proxy for v-noise dispersion → pushes the algorithm toward low arcs (short TOF).
  • \(\theta\) noise (servo jitter ±1°): near the apex of a high arc, \(\partial x / \partial \theta \to 0\) (the apex tangent is vertical), whereas at low arcs \(\Delta\theta\) translates one-for-one into \(\Delta x\). For θ-noise dispersion, TOF² is a proxy pointing the wrong way → the algorithm should pick high arcs.

w_T = 200 let the TOF² term dominate the cost and pushed the algorithm into the low-arc region, amplifying θ-noise dispersion (the root cause of the original V3's 1.19 m spread at long range). Burst-simulation check (d=3 m, 100 balls, ±3% v + ±1° θ jitter):

V3 (w_T=200)48.89°7.26 m/s97.8%0.96 m
V3.1 (w_T=1.1)62.99°7.18 m/s100%0.51 m

The V3.1 fix: physics calibration of w_T (vortex shedding)

w_T should not be an arbitrary constant. Its correct semantics is unmodeled stochastic noise — random disturbances the simulation doesn't model (mechanical jitter is already modeled explicitly in the burst sim and shouldn't be penalized twice by the cost). The dominant unmodeled stochastic noise is vortex shedding (the Kármán vortex street).

In flight, Re = \(v \cdot d / \nu\) = (6 m/s)(0.15 m) / (1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s) ≈ 6×10⁴, in the subcritical vortex-shedding regime. Vortices shed alternately from the ball's wake (Strouhal number St ≈ 0.18, shedding frequency ≈ 8 Hz), producing a random lateral lift force whose accumulated displacement grows with TOF.

Darbois-Texier et al. (New J. Phys. 18, 073027, 2016, "Physics of knuckleballs") derive the lateral-deviation scaling law under broadband lateral forcing:

$$\sigma_{x,\text{vortex}} \propto \text{TOF}^2$$

Quantitatively, for a smooth sphere at subcritical Re with \(C_l' \approx 0.07\) (RMS lateral force coefficient, Norman & McKeon, Exp. Fluids 51, 2011):

$$\sigma_{x,\text{vortex}}(T) \approx 0.04 \cdot T^2 \;\text{m} \quad (T\text{ in s})$$

For our T ≈ 1 s flights → σ_x ≈ 4 cm. Against the hex effective R = 45.5 cm that is 9% — small but nonzero.

Cost calibration: balance the w_T · T² term against the w_V · v² term at the σ/R scale:

$$w_T \cdot T_{\text{design}}^2 \approx w_V \cdot v_{\text{design}}^2 \cdot \left(\frac{\sigma_{x,\text{vortex}}}{R_{\text{eff}}}\right)^2$$

Substituting \(T_{\text{design}} = 1\) s, \(v_{\text{design}} = 7\) m/s, \(w_V = 3\), \(\sigma_{x,\text{vortex}} = 0.04\) m, \(R_{\text{eff}} = 0.455\) m:

$$w_T = 3 \cdot 49 \cdot (0.04/0.455)^2 = 1.14 \;\to\; \mathbf{w_T \approx 1.1}$$

Precision statement: the 4 cm estimate of σx,vortex itself carries a ±factor-2 uncertainty (Cl' RMS ranges 0.05–0.10 in the sport-ball literature; the 0.07 above is the midpoint). So any \(w_T\) between 0.3 and 4 is "physics-consistent" — do not read it as four significant figures. Since the V3.1 plateau yields essentially the same solutions for w_T ∈ [0, 40], this uncertainty doesn't affect the final hood/v choice; it only affects the semantic claim of what the TOF² term represents in the cost.

Corollary (scaling): halve the ball's mass, double the distance, or halve the hex, and w_T scales as \((\sigma/R)^2\) (half the mass → σ_x doubles → w_T ×4; double the distance → T doubles → σ_x ×4 → w_T ×16). This FRC 2026 configuration happens to sit in a physical sweet spot where vortex shedding is nearly negligible — which is why w_T is so small. Darbois-Texier et al. state this explicitly: sport balls have \(\rho_{\text{ball}}/\rho_{\text{air}} \gg 1\) (ours is ~100); a heavy ball isn't pushed around by its vortices, so the vortex-street effect is naturally suppressed.

Within the V3.1 plateau, any w_T ∈ [0, 40] yields solutions in the same region (hood differing by a few degrees); w_T = 1.1 makes the TOF² term a light tie-breaker within the plateau, physically pegged to the vortex-shedding magnitude rather than an ungrounded guess.

V4: 4414-style robust center PRINCIPLED

Core observation: maximizing hit probability under \((v, \theta)\) noise is approximately equivalent to finding the point in the valid region farthest from the boundary. No weights, no measuring \(\sigma_v\), σ_θ — just maximize the worst-case noise tolerance directly.

Algorithm: (1) an \(N \times N\) grid sweep builds the valid mask; (2) distance_transform_cdt(metric='chessboard'), each cell's value = the largest symmetric tolerance-box radius (in cells); (3) take the argmax — the robust center.

L∞ rather than L²: L² finds the largest inscribed circle, L∞ the largest inscribed square, which corresponds exactly to a symmetric \(\pm\Delta v \times \pm\Delta\theta\) tolerance box.

V4r: scipy continuous polish ~+5 s per distance

At grid_n=40, the valid banana is sometimes only 2–3 cells wide, causing artifacts. Starting from the grid winner, run a bounded Nelder-Mead within a local \([\pm 0.8 \text{ m/s}] \times [\pm 6°]\) box, maximizing continuous Chebyshev tolerance (bisecting \(\Delta v_\pm\), \(\Delta\theta_\pm\) in 4 directions).

V4r2: energy tiebreak FINAL

V4r still jittered across the distance sweep: the valid banana sometimes holds two fat plateaus (high arc + low arc), and which one wins the chess-distance max flips under tiny changes between neighboring distances → the hood angle hops back and forth between ~50° and ~65°.

User preference: "when the robust centers are about the same, pick the one with the lower flywheel speed" (energy saving).

Implementation: replace the grid argmax with a toleranced tiebreak. Among all cells whose chess distance is \(\ge\) max − 1 cell, sort ascending by v_exit and take the lowest. The core code:

def find_robust_center(mask, near_max_cells=1):
    dist = distance_transform_cdt(mask, metric='chessboard')
    max_dist = dist.max()
    threshold = max(1, max_dist - near_max_cells)
    candidates = np.argwhere(dist >= threshold)
    # ascending sort: v index first (lower v wins), then θ index
    sorted_idx = candidates[np.lexsort((candidates[:, 1], candidates[:, 0]))]
    i, j = sorted_idx[0]
    return int(i), int(j), float(dist[i, j])

The scipy polish also gains a tiny v-preference term (\(1\text{e-}4 \cdot v\)) so the continuous stage leans toward low v as well.

Effect: the sweep curves smooth out markedly (Fig. 4), and RPM drops across the board.

5. Polynomial Fit + RIO Deployment

The V4r2 robust optimizer's solutions are inside the valid region but not necessarily at the hub center (they only need to be within ±0.455 m, and may sit off-center deliberately to maximize tolerance). Fitting the polynomial to the V4r2 sweep directly would put the dispersion center off the hub center once deployed. So the final deployment fits the polynomial to the V3 heuristic sweep (centered landing as a hard constraint + the same foam-ball energy weights):

  1. A 15-row table wastes RIO flash, and lookups still need distance interpolation
  2. Interpolating a jittery table isn't smooth → command jumps → the flywheel control loop chatters
The V3 sweep (centered + cost-minimized) is naturally smooth and lands exactly at the hub center at every d (max offset < 1 mm). Its polynomial fit RMSE is also an order of magnitude below V4r2's (hood 0.4° vs 1.85°). This is the deployment practice Team 4414 spells out in their 2026 software docs — 3 float coefficients on the RIO + one multiply-add per output (< 1 μs).

Why the primary output is surface speed, not RPM: what ships to robot code is the flywheel surface speed \(v_\text{fly}\) (m/s), which is wheel-diameter-independent and portable across hardware (a friend's new robot may use a different wheel). It comes from the ball exit speed corrected for wheel–ball slip:

$$v_\text{fly} = \frac{v_\text{ball}}{1 - s}, \qquad s = 0.18\ (\text{flywheel slip\_factor})$$

The slip factor \(s\) is itself an estimate — exactly the uncertainty the single field knob kSpeed absorbs (see §5.2). To convert back to RPM with your own wheel diameter \(D\): \(\text{rpm} = v_\text{fly}/(\pi D)\times 60\). At this project's 0.1016 m wheel that equals the flywheel RPM column above, and equals the RPMs shown in §4's tables and Alpha Sim (e.g. \(d=3\) m: \(v_\text{fly}=8.70\) m/s \(\leftrightarrow\) 1636 RPM), so the two sets of numbers are consistent.

The same coefficients are also stored machine-readably in lookup_polynomial.json (all four sets — v_ball, flywheel surface speed, reference RPM — identical to the §5.2 functions), for roboRIO code or tooling to read as needed.

5.2 Deployment snippet (the delivered function)

This is the function body handed to whoever writes the new shooter code: input distance \(d\), output hood angle and flywheel surface speed; the only field knob is kSpeed. WPILib (Java 17):

public final class ShooterAim {

    // Exit height (m): the coefficients were generated at this height and it is
    // baked into the polynomials. Changing this constant at runtime does NOTHING.
    // To change the height, rerun
    //   python scripts/generate_coefficients.py --shooter-height <h>
    // and paste the new a/b/c below. The only live knob at runtime is kSpeed.
    public static final double SHOOTER_HEIGHT_M = 0.50;

    // Valid fit range (m). Outside it the polynomial extrapolation is untrustworthy
    // (the hood curve is U-shaped and bends back upward).
    public static final double D_MIN = 1.5, D_MAX = 5.0;

    // ── The one field-calibration knob ── flywheel surface-speed gain, start at 1.0.
    //   Falling short → increase (e.g. 1.03); overshooting → decrease (e.g. 0.97).
    //   One number absorbs slip / ball compression / battery voltage / air-model error.
    public static double kSpeed = 1.00;

    public static double hoodAngleDeg(double d) {
        d = clamp(d);
        return 0.887787 * d*d - 10.549949 * d + 86.441900;
    }

    // Surface speed (m/s). To RPM: rpm = v / (Math.PI * wheelDiameterD) * 60.
    public static double flywheelSpeedMps(double d) {
        d = clamp(d);
        return kSpeed * (0.016170 * d*d + 0.717698 * d + 6.404844);
    }

    private static double clamp(double d) { return Math.max(D_MIN, Math.min(D_MAX, d)); }
    private ShooterAim() {}
}

Python equivalent (exact same coefficients):

SHOOTER_HEIGHT_M = 0.50          # exit height the coefficients were generated at (baked in, not a runtime input)
D_MIN, D_MAX = 1.5, 5.0
K_SPEED = 1.00                   # ← the one field knob: falling short → raise, overshooting → lower

def _clamp(d): return max(D_MIN, min(D_MAX, d))

def hood_angle_deg(d):
    d = _clamp(d)
    return 0.887787*d*d - 10.549949*d + 86.441900

def flywheel_speed_mps(d):       # surface speed m/s; rpm = v / (pi * wheel_diameter) * 60
    d = _clamp(d)
    return K_SPEED * (0.016170*d*d + 0.717698*d + 6.404844)

def aim(d):
    return hood_angle_deg(d), flywheel_speed_mps(d)

One evaluation takes < 1 μs — a billion times cheaper than running the robust solver (seconds to minutes) every cycle. Design point: the hood angle follows the fitted curve only, no knob; the speed follows the fitted curve × kSpeed. Field calibration = shoot a few balls, nudge kSpeed until the landing is centered — one parameter, not all of them.

5.3 Fit accuracy analysis

  • hood RMSE 0.10°: hood servos are typically ±0.5° themselves, mechanical assembly ±1° — the polynomial error is far below that noise → never the limiting factor
  • v_exit RMSE 0.006 m/s: ~0.08% relative error. Kraken RPM control + slip variation dwarf this → the polynomial error sits below the noise floor
  • RPM RMSE 1.3: 0.07% of 1500–2000 RPM. Equally negligible
  • V3.1 vs V3 fit accuracy: the V3.1 sweep is far smoother than the original V3 (the high-arc plateau is closer to monotonic in (d, hood) space than the low-arc one), so the same 2nd-order polynomial fits V3.1 with 1/4 the RMSE (hood: 0.10° vs 0.40°)
  • Three-axis fit consistency: v_exit and RPM are nearly perfectly linear (residuals ±0.1 m/s / ±25 RPM); hood is U-shaped, and 2nd order suffices to ride the V4r2 mean trend

5.4 Difference from 4414

4414's polynomial has two input variables: distance \(d\) and the robot's radial velocity \(v_\text{radial}\) ("how fast the robot is moving toward the hub"). That lets a moving robot instantly compute "where the hub will be when the ball lands". We currently fit distance only, because Stage 1 ignores robot translation. Once Stage 2 adds it, the polynomial just grows to two variables:

\(\text{hood}(d, v_r) = a_1 d^2 + a_2 v_r^2 + a_3 d v_r + a_4 d + a_5 v_r + a_6\) (6 coefficients).
